What is an IEP?

An Individualized Education Program, or IEP, is a written plan for a child who qualifies for special education services at a U.S. public school. It is created under a federal law called the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA).

An IEP usually describes:

  • your child's strengths and needs,
  • yearly goals,
  • the services and supports the school will provide,
  • and how progress will be shared with you.

Autism is one of the disability categories listed in IDEA. Parents are members of the IEP team, so your knowledge of your child matters at the meeting.

Rules vary. IDEA is a federal law, but states and school districts have their own timelines and forms. Check with your state education department or school for the rules that apply to you.

Learn more from official sources: the U.S. Department of Education IDEA website and the Center for Parent Information and Resources.

Questions parents ask

These answers are general information. Rules can differ by state and district.

What is an IEP?

An Individualized Education Program (IEP) is a written plan for a child who qualifies for special education services under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). It describes the child's needs, goals, services and supports.

How do I ask the school to evaluate my child?

You can ask in writing. Many parents send a dated letter or email to the principal or special education director and keep a copy. The kit includes a template letter you can adapt.

How long does the school have to evaluate my child?

Under IDEA, the initial evaluation generally must be finished within 60 days after you give consent, unless your state sets a different timeline. Some states use a different number of days, so check your state education department.

Who is at an IEP meeting?

The IEP team usually includes the parents, at least one general education teacher, at least one special education teacher or provider, a school representative, and someone who can explain evaluation results. Parents may also invite people who know the child well.

What happens at the meeting?

The team talks about your child's strengths, needs and goals, and what services and supports might help. You can ask questions, share what you see at home and ask for time to review before you agree.

Do I have to agree with everything?

No. You can ask questions, ask for changes and ask for time to think. If you still disagree, IDEA describes options such as mediation, a state complaint or a due process hearing. The school must give you a notice explaining your procedural safeguards. For help, consider a parent center, an advocate or an attorney.

How often is an IEP reviewed?

At least once a year. A child is also generally re-evaluated at least every three years, unless the parent and school agree it is not needed. You can ask for a meeting sooner if you have concerns.

Does the kit work in every state?

The kit is built around the federal IDEA process, so it is general. Your state or district may have different forms, names and timelines. Check your state education department's website for specifics.

Is this legal advice?

No. The kit is an educational resource. It does not replace advice from a licensed attorney, an advocate or your school's special education team, and it does not guarantee any outcome.
